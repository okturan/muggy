How Muggy works

Why not just temperature?

Because temperature doesn't tell you how the air feels. 30° in dry air is a pleasant day; 27° in soaked air is a slog. The difference is how easily your sweat evaporates — evaporation is how your body cools itself, and wet air slows it down.

The honest measure of that is the dew point: the temperature the air would have to cool to before its moisture starts condensing. The higher it is, the more water the air is already carrying, and the worse your sweat works. Relative humidity can't tell you this on its own — 100% humidity on a cold morning is fog, not misery.

Muggy reads the dew point, sorts it into a comfort band, and shows you the band. The number itself stays backstage, because "dew point 21°" means nothing to most people and "muggy" means everything.

The six bands

The thresholds are the ones WeatherSpark made familiar, converted to Celsius:

BandDew pointHow it feels
drybelow 13°Crisp. Skin and lips feel it.
comfortable13 – 16°The good stuff. Go outside.
humid16 – 18°Noticeable, not a problem.
muggy18 – 21°Shirts stick. Shade helps.
oppressive21 – 24°Sweat won't dry. Take it slow.
miserable24° and upStay in. Find the AC.

What is humidex?

The band tells you how sticky the air is — but sticky air at 34° and the same sticky air at 24° are very different afternoons. Humidex puts the two together: it combines the temperature and the dew point into one number that says how heavy the combination actually is on your body.

It was created by Canadian meteorologists in 1965 and is what Environment Canada still uses for heat warnings. Read it like a "feels like" temperature in °C: a humidex of 36 means the heat-plus-moisture load on your body is roughly what 36° of dry heat would be.

This is why the same "muggy" band feels easier in the evening: the moisture hasn't gone anywhere, but the temperature half of the sum has dropped and the sun has stopped adding to it. The card under the score says exactly that.

Two better-known cousins exist. The US heat index answers the same question with different maths (Muggy uses humidex because it is built directly from the dew point). WBGT, used for sport and work safety, additionally measures sunlight and wind — it needs instruments a weather API can't fully replace, so Muggy reports the sun separately instead of guessing at a WBGT.

"Is this normal?"

A band alone doesn't tell you whether to be surprised. So Muggy compares right now against ten years of history for this exact place and time of year — every hour recorded within a week either side of today's date — and tells you where the moment sits: stickier than 93% of days, or squarely normal. The coloured bar is the local climate at a glance: one segment per band, sized by how much of the past decade fell in it.

"When will it get better?"

The forecast already knows when the air eases off, so Muggy says it outright: the first stretch in the next 24 hours that is a band better than what you're standing in, and where it bottoms out — muggy from 22:00, easing to dry by 03:00. When nothing better is coming, it says that instead of dressing up a least-bad hour as advice.
